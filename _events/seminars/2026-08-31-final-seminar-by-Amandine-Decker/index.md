## Abstract

Dialogue constitutes a fundamental mode of human interaction and has become increasingly central in
natural language processing with the growing use of conversational AI systems both in research and in
practical applications. However, dialogue is a heterogeneous phenomenon encompassing a wide range
of forms, arising from differences in participant configurations, interaction contexts, or communication
media. This diversity creates challenges for the study of dialogue data, particularly in selecting suitable
resources, defining the scope and limitations of our studies, and establishing meaningful evaluation
methodologies. A principled characterisation of dialogue is therefore necessary to account for its variations
and to support the design of robust computational approaches.
Among the phenomena that cut across different forms of dialogue, topical organisation plays a central
role. The structure and evolution of topics shape conversational dynamics and are themselves influenced
by the type of interaction being considered. Studying such phenomena requires moving beyond local
conversational patterns and adopting a broader, macro-level perspective on dialogue, allowing different
interacting processes and structures to be examined jointly.
This thesis investigates three main research questions: (1) how dialogue can be characterised in a
way that accounts for its variability while informing the selection of suitable resources and evaluation
methods; (2) whether topical structure can be automatically extracted from dialogue data, with a particular
focus on open-ended multi-party conversations, that are more complex but also very common in
real-world interactions; and (3) whether topical structure can be leveraged as a discriminative signal for
differentiating dialogue types. Through these questions, the thesis aims to contribute both analytical and
methodological perspectives toward a broader understanding of dialogue structure and variation.
